Irish dance words, explained

134 words you'll hear at class and at the feis, what they mean, why they matter, and how to say the Irish ones.

Competitions

Feis

Say “fesh”. A local Irish dance competition, usually one day. Most of your weekends will revolve around these.

Feiseanna

Say “FESH-uh-nuh”. Plural of feis. You'll see it on calendars and registration sites.

Oireachtas (Regionals)

Say “oh-ROCK-tus”. The regional championship, held once a year. Qualifying dancers compete for places at Nationals and Worlds.

Nationals

National championship (for example North American Nationals or the Great Britain Championships) Usually needs a qualifying result for championship dancers.

All-Irelands

The All-Ireland Championships, held in Ireland. A major championship open to dancers from abroad.

Worlds (Oireachtas Rince na Cruinne)CLRG only

Say “oh-ROCK-tus RIN-kuh nuh KRIN-yuh”. The CLRG World Championships. Qualification only, usually through a regional or national event.

Grades (grade competitions)

All the levels below championship (Beginner 1, Beginner 2, Novice, Open Prizewinner). Each dance is its own competition, split by level and age. Where most dancers spend their first years. Not the same as grade exams.

Championship (Prelim, Open)

Prelim (PC) and Open (OC) competitions. PC is 2 rounds (soft shoe, hard shoe); OC adds a 3rd, the set round (own choice set). Judged by a panel, with one overall place. Unlike grades, where every dance is its own competition with its own award.

Round placing

In a championship, where your dancer placed in one round. One judge from the panel is picked at random for each round, and that judge's placings are given to every dancer for that round. Round places can look very different from the overall place, because the other judges may have seen it differently.

Overall place

A dancer's final championship result, from every judge's scores for every round added together. It isn't worked out from the round placings. This is the place that counts for awards, moving up and qualifying.

Marshalling (side stage, marshal)

Lining dancers up in order before they go on stage. A marshal is the volunteer who does it. Get to the stage early; if your dancer misses their turn, they may not get to dance.

Recall

Being called back after the first rounds to dance the final round. A recall is itself an achievement worth celebrating.

Placement (podium)

Finishing high enough to be called up for an award. How many places are awarded depends on the size of the competition.

Raw score (marks)

Points each judge gives. What the score tracker can log.

Irish points

Placings converted into points so different judges can be combined. Explains why the winner isn't always the dancer with the highest raw marks.

Ceili

Say “KAY-lee”. A group dance, or a social dance night. Team dances at feiseanna and championships.

Figure dance (team dance)

A choreographed group dance, often 4 to 16 dancers. Team practice adds to the schedule.

Stage (adjudicator's table)

Where each competition is held at a feis. Check the stage number in the program.

Competitor number (competitor card)

The number your dancer wears. Pin it to the costume; you'll use it to look up results.

Results posting

Results on paper boards at the venue, or published online afterwards. What parents photograph for the score tracker.

Syllabus

The list of competitions, ages and fees at a feis. Read it before you register.

U8 / 15O (age groups, under, over)

How age groups are written on a syllabus. U means under, so U8 is under 8. O means and over, so 15O is 15 and over. Each feis says which date it uses to work out age, so check before you enter.

Light round (soft shoe round)

The soft shoe round of a championship: a reel or a slip jig. Usually the first round your dancer dances.

Heavy round (hard shoe round)

The hard shoe round of a championship: a treble jig or a hornpipe. Usually the second round.

Specials (special competitions, trophy dance)

Extra competitions at a feis that don't count toward moving up, such as a trophy dance, a traditional set or a treble reel. A fun way to dance more, but they add to your entry fees.

School feis

A small feis that only the host school's own dancers can enter. A friendly place for a first competition.

Blackout feis

A feis where solo dresses and fancy costumes aren't allowed, and dancers wear simple outfits, usually black. Cheaper and quicker to get ready for. The syllabus will say if it's one.

Parade of Champions

A walk-on at the end of a feis where the winners of each age group come out and dance a step in turn. It isn't judged. A proud moment if your dancer won their competition.

Perpetual trophy

A trophy the winner keeps for a while and then hands back, usually after a year, so it can be won again. Look after it and return it on time; some feiseanna ask for a deposit.

Sash (belt)

An award worn across the body, often given to the top dancers in a competition. Boys sometimes get a belt instead. Great for photos, and a keepsake.

Blocking

When a dancer keeps moving into a spot in front of another dancer, so the judge sees them more. Teachers coach dancers to use the stage well and not to block others.

Fleadh

Say “flah”. A festival of Irish music, sometimes with dancing or dance competitions. A fun day out that's different from a feis.

Majors (major championships)

The big championships above a local feis, such as the Oireachtas, Nationals, the All-Irelands and Worlds. They run over several days and usually need a qualifying result. The teacher usually enters dancers for these, and travel is often involved.

NAIDC (North American Nationals, NANs)CLRG only

The North American Irish Dance Championships, held each summer and hosted in turn by the North American regions. A second route to qualifying for Worlds, after the Oireachtas.

World qualifier (WQ)CLRG only

A dancer who has earned a place at Worlds. Most qualify at their Oireachtas, and some at Nationals or the All-Irelands. Qualifying places are limited, so it's a big moment.

National qualifier (NQ)CLRG only

A dancer who has earned a place at Nationals in their solo competition, usually through an Oireachtas result. In North America, check the deadlines with your teacher.

World medal holder (WMH)CLRG only

A dancer who placed high enough at Worlds to win a medal. They're usually qualified for the next year's Worlds without needing to qualify again.

First Feis (Tír na nÓg)

Say “teer nuh NOGE”. A first competition for the very youngest or newest dancers, with short dances and a prize for everyone. A gentle, low-pressure way to start. Not every feis offers it.

Adult competitions (adult grades, adult championship)

Competitions for people who started dancing as adults, or who haven't competed as a child for several years. Each organization sets its own age and eligibility rules.

MastersCLRG only

Adult competitions, in some North American regions, for adults who danced as children. Only some regions offer them, so check your regional syllabus.

Shows (Riverdance, Lord of the Dance)

Performances, from local St. Patrick's Day shows to touring stage shows like Riverdance (1994) and Lord of the Dance (1996), which made Irish dance famous worldwide. Many schools perform in the community, and some dancers go on to dance professionally.

Bun Grád / Tús GrádCLRG only

Say “bun grawd / toos grawd”. The CLRG rulebook's names for the first two grades (Beginner and Advanced Beginner) Special costume, make-up and filming rules apply to these grades.

RestylingCLRG only

A 3-month wait before a dancer who changes school can compete again. Plan around it if you're thinking of changing schools.

Bun GrádAn Comhdháil only

Say “bun grawd”. An Comhdháil's beginner grades: Beginners A, B and C. Win a dance with 10 or more dancers to move up.

Meán GrádAn Comhdháil only

Say “MAWN grawd”. Pre-Open, the middle grade. Danced in age groups; win with 5 or more dancers to move up.

Ard GrádAn Comhdháil only

Say “AWRD grawd”. Open, the top solo grade. Needed before entering a Championship.

RestylingAn Comhdháil only

A 3-month wait before a dancer who moves into or within An Comhdháil can compete. Starts when the new teacher's branch gets the registration form.

Provincial Championship (Qualifier)An Comhdháil only

The championship a dancer must dance to qualify for the World Championships. Dancers may only enter one each season.

Cup competitionAn Comhdháil only

A special competition at beginner or Pre-Open level; the winner moves up in that dance. Only one cup per grade per feis.

NCH (Non-Championship Holder)An Comhdháil only

A competition for dancers who haven't won a Championship, with a light and a heavy dance. Winning makes the dancer Open in those dances.

PremiershipWIDA only

A WIDA competition where a dancer does two dances and the scores are added together (for example reel and light jig) Your dancer can enter one premiership per feis, at the grade they're in for most of their dances.

Jump 2-3WIDA only

A first competition for the youngest WIDA dancers: 16 jump 2-3s or promenade steps. From 2027 it's for under-7s only.

YafersWIDA only

The online system used to enter every WIDA feis. Entries are non-refundable once paid.

FéileCRN only

Say “FAY-luh”. A festival or competition. CRN and others use it for many of their local competitions. A féile works like a feis for your dancer.

Feis HostCRN only

The website CRN uses for feis entries (crn.feishost.com) Upcoming CRN feiseanna are listed there.

A Award / B AwardCRDM only

CRDM competitions in front of 1 judge for beginner dancers: A for dancers with 3 or more dances in Beginner A, B for 2 or more in Beginner B. Your dancer must also dance at least 2 solos that day to enter.

Intermediate AwardCRDM only

A CRDM award competition in front of 1 judge, for dancers who haven't won it, or a Preliminary, Premiership or Open Championship. Winning it moves your dancer up in the Award and in that solo dance.

Premiership ChampionshipCRDM only

A CRDM championship for dancers aged 9 and up who haven't won an Open Championship: slip jig and a traditional set (boys dance a reel) in front of 3 judges. Winning it counts like a Preliminary Championship win.

1 Adjudicator ChampionshipCRDM only

A CRDM championship with one judge. Winning it makes your dancer Open in both dances, and it's part of qualifying for the CRDM Worlds.

Championship pointsCRDM only

CRDM's points for championship placings: 100 for 1st, 75 for 2nd, 65 for 3rd, down to 1 point for 50th. How CRDM turns championship placings into points.

Dances, shoes & costume

Soft shoe

Light, lace-up dances: reel, light jig, slip jig, single jig. The first dances most beginners learn.

Hard shoe (heavy shoe, jig shoe)

Dances in shoes with fiberglass tips and heels that make rhythm: treble jig, hornpipe, treble reel. A second pair of shoes, usually bought a bit later.

Ghillies (pumps, pomps)

Say “GILL-eez”. Soft, laced shoes worn by girls and women. Need replacing as feet grow; check the fit with the teacher.

Reel shoes

Soft shoes for boys, like a jazz shoe with a heel that clicks. The boys' equivalent of ghillies.

Reel

A fast soft shoe dance in 2/4 or 4/4 time. Usually the first competition dance.

Light jig

A quick soft shoe dance in 6/8. Often danced at beginner levels.

Slip jig

A graceful soft shoe dance in 9/8. Mostly danced by girls.

Single jig (hop jig)

A soft shoe jig in 6/8 or 12/8. Whether it's offered varies by region.

Treble jig (heavy jig)

Say “TREB-ul”. A slow, heavy shoe jig. A core hard shoe dance.

Hornpipe

A heavy shoe dance in 2/4 or 4/4. A core hard shoe dance.

Set dance

A hard shoe dance to a set tune. There are traditional sets and own choice sets. Appears from the middle levels upward.

Traditional set (trad set)

A set dance with set steps that every school learns, like St. Patrick's Day or The Blackbird. Danced at the lower and middle grades.

Own choice set (open set, contemporary set, non-traditional set)

A set dance where the dancer picks the tune and dances their teacher's own steps. This is the set danced in championship rounds, not a traditional set.

Steps

Short choreographed sections of a dance, each about 8 bars a side. Teachers give new steps as dancers move up.

School costume (class costume, school dress)

The dress or outfit in your school's colors. Usually worn until a dancer moves into a solo dress.

Solo dress

A dancer's own competition dress. One of the biggest costs; rules govern when it's allowed.

Wig

A curled wig worn in competition, usually by girls. Often from the middle levels; check age and level rules.

Poodle socks

Long white ribbed socks. Held up with sock glue.

Sock glue

Roll-on adhesive that keeps socks in place. Bring it to every feis.

Treble reel

A hard shoe dance to fast reel music, packed with rhythm. Popular in shows. At a feis it's usually a special or fun competition, not a main dance.

Traditional speed (fast jig, fast hornpipe)

A treble jig or hornpipe danced at a quicker speed, used by newer dancers. Your dancer's level decides which speed they dance, so check the syllabus.

Contrasting setCLRG only

A set dance in the opposite rhythm to the dancer's hard shoe round: a jig-time set after a hornpipe, or a hornpipe set after a treble jig. Some major championships ask for it in the set round; local feiseanna usually don't.

Two-hand

A team dance for a pair of dancers. Often a dancer's first team competition.

Eight-hand (four-hand, six-hand)

A céilí team named for its size: 4, 6 or 8 dancers, dancing a traditional céilí dance. Teams don't have to be evenly split between boys and girls.

Dance drama

A team dance that tells a story, with character costumes and props. Usually a special event at a major or a large feis.

Blackout costume (blackout)

A plain all-black outfit worn instead of a costume, with simpler hair and make-up. A cheaper option, but whether it's allowed depends on your organization and the dancer's level.

Buckles

Decorations, often sparkly, on the front strap of hard shoes. Small and easy to lose, so pack a spare.

Headband (tiara, crown)

A decorated band that holds a wig in place and matches the dress. Often bought with a solo dress or wig.

Waistcoat (vest, cummerbund)

Boys' costume: usually trousers, a shirt and tie, and a vest or waistcoat, sometimes with a sash (cummerbund) in the school colors. Boys' costumes cost much less than dresses, but still need to fit well.

Rince

Say “RIN-kuh”. The Irish word for dance. It's in the names of schools, organizations and events, like Oireachtas Rince na Cruinne.

Sean nós

Say “shan nohss”. An older, more relaxed style of Irish dance, close to the floor and often made up on the spot. The name means old style. You may see it at music sessions and festivals rather than a feis.

Festival dancing

A traditional style of Irish dance with simpler costumes and its own competitions, mainly in Northern Ireland. If you hear of festival dancing, it's a different style from feis dancing.

Lilting

Singing the rhythm of the steps out loud, like da-da-dum, to teach or remember them. You'll hear dancers lilting their steps in the car or kitchen.

Elastics (shoe straps, white tape)

Bands or straps that go over the shoes to keep them on while dancing. Pack spares, because a shoe coming off can cost a place.

Kick pants (bloomers, dance briefs)

Shorts worn under a dance dress or skirt. Often need to match the dress or be a set color.

Kilt

A pleated skirt-style garment that was the usual costume for boys and men for much of the 1900s. You'll see it in older photos; most boys wear trousers today.

Ceol

Say “kyole”. The Irish word for music. It often appears in school, camp and event names.

Damhsa

Say “DOW-suh”. Another Irish word for dance, like rince. You'll see it in names, such as CRN's teacher diploma.

Comhghairdeas

Say “co-HAR-juss”. Irish for congratulations. You may see it on medals, sashes and certificates.

Tan

Spray or bottled tan for legs. Rules on makeup and tan vary by age and level.

Modern setWIDA only

A set dance to modern music, used as a third round in WIDA Open Championships. Only recalled dancers dance it.

Moves & technique

Turnout

Feet and legs turned outward from the hips, so the toes point out to the sides. Judges look closely at it; it's one of the first things teachers correct.

Carriage

How the upper body is held: straight back, still shoulders, arms close to the sides. Good carriage makes the footwork stand out.

Cross (crossed feet)

Legs crossed so one foot sits in front of the other, with the feet turned out. Tight crossing is a sign of good technique, and judges watch for it.

Treble (batter, rally)

Say “TREB-ul”. A quick brush and tap of the foot that makes fast rhythm sounds, mostly in hard shoe. You'll hear teachers count trebles in class.

Click

Hitting the heels of the hard shoes together in the air to make a sharp sound. A crowd favorite in hard shoe steps.

Cut

Bringing the foot up sharply in front of the body, as high as the dancer can. Shows strength and control, especially in soft shoe.

Toe stand

Balancing up on the tips of the hard shoes, sometimes walking or turning on them. An advanced move that needs strong ankles; some organizations limit it for younger dancers.

Leap (bird, stag leap)

A big jump across the stage. A bird has one leg straight out and the other tucked under; a stag has the front leg bent. Leaps add height and drama to soft shoe steps.

Rising step (jig step, rise and grind)

A common soft shoe step pattern: a hop, then hopping back, then hopping back again. One of the first patterns beginners learn.

Sevens (sidestep)

A travelling step that goes sideways or on a diagonal over seven counts. Used in both solo steps and team dances.

Threes (hop 1-2-3, skips)

A basic three-count step, done in place or travelling, that later grows into leaps. A building block of almost every dance.

Lead around (lead round)

A short circling section at the start of a step or dance. Beginners often learn this first.

Drum (roll, drumroll)

A very fast toe-heel-toe pattern in hard shoes. Makes the rattling sound you hear in hard shoe.

Rocks

Crossing the feet and rocking side to side on the ankles. A classic move that needs strong ankles.

Flutters (butterflies, scissors)

A jump straight up with pointed feet, where the legs cross past each other two or more times in the air. A jump that looks impressive.

Point and bow

Pointing the foot and bowing to the judge and the musician at the end of a dance. It's polite stage manners, and judges notice it.

Hop back

A small hop backward on one foot, often linking two moves. One of the basics in early steps.

People & organizations

CLRG (An Coimisiún)CLRG only

Say “un KUM-ish-oon”. An Coimisiún le Rincí Gaelacha, the largest Irish dance organization, based in Dublin. Sets the rules your dancer competes under.

TCRGCLRG onlyCRDM only

A certified teacher. Mostly CLRG, but CRDM uses the same title for its own teachers. Your dancer competes as their TCRG's pupil. Families register for regular feiseanna themselves, but the TCRG registers dancers for majors like the Oireachtas, Nationals and Worlds.

ADCRGCLRG onlyCRDM only

A certified adjudicator (judge). Mostly CLRG, but CRDM uses the same title for its own judges. The people scoring your dancer.

SDCRGCLRG only

A certified CLRG examiner, who can run grade exams. Mostly relevant to teachers.

ADCRG-BGCLRG only

A CLRG teacher and judge who can also examine the first 10 grade exams, but isn't a full examiner (SDCRG) Mostly relevant if your school does grade exams.

Adjudicator

A judge. In grades one judge places each dance. Championships use a panel: usually 3, 5 at major championships, and 6 at the All-Irelands with 5 counting.

RegionCLRG only

A geographic area under CLRG with its own regional body and Oireachtas. Your region decides your Oireachtas and some local rules.

IDTANACLRG only

Irish Dance Teachers' Association of North America, the body for CLRG teachers in North America. Its regions run the North American Oireachtas events.

Grade exam (Grád)CLRG only

Say “grawd”. Optional CLRG exams in dance skills and knowledge. Some schools encourage them; separate from competitions.

TMRFCLRG onlyWIDA onlyCRDM only

A teacher qualification. In CLRG it's a céilí teacher's certificate, for teaching team dances only. WIDA and CRDM use the title for their own teachers too. Teachers with only TMRF can still compete themselves.

ODCRNCRN only

A fully qualified CRN teacher (Oide Damhsa diploma) The CRN version of a teacher's title.

TMCRNCRN only

A CRN teacher who has passed Part 1 of the ODCRN teacher diploma. A certified, part-qualified CRN teacher.

ADCRNCRN only

A CRN adjudicator (judge) The people scoring your dancer at CRN feiseanna.

Open platform (independent, unaffiliated)

A feis or organization open to dancers from any organization, or a teacher or school that isn't part of any organization. Check with your teacher first: some organizations limit which open platform events their dancers can enter.

The SplitCLRG onlyAn Comhdháil only

The 1969 split when An Comhdháil separated from CLRG to become its own organization. Explains why there are two big organizations with similar names and rules.

Feis committee

The volunteers running a particular feis. Contact them about entries, schedules and results.
